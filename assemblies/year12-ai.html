01 / AI in everyday study

AI is changing how we learn.

Used as a learning tool

Copilot can explain ideas, create practice questions and offer feedback.

The student still does the thinking.

Used as a shortcut

AI can produce polished answers without building understanding.

Finishing work is not the same as learning it.

Same technology. Two very different ways to use it.

Help or shortcut?

Asking for an explanation can build your understanding. Asking for a finished essay might get a task done without helping you learn anything.

Try this: After using Copilot, write three things you have learnt without looking back at its answer.

02 / What does generative mean?

It generates new content.

Unlike a traditional search engine, a generative AI model can construct a response, one small piece at a time.

01 / Training

Learn patterns

During training, a model adjusts billions of numerical parameters to improve its predictions.

02 / Your prompt

Provide context

Your words are split into tokens — often words or pieces of words — that the model can process.

03 / Generation

Predict and repeat

The model estimates possible next tokens, chooses one, then uses the updated sequence to continue.

✳It's generating a response, not necessarily retrieving a checked fact.

What is generative AI?

Generative AI can create new text, images and code from patterns learnt during training. It is not simply searching a database for a ready-made answer.

Try this: Remember: training adjusts the model's parameters; using a model normally generates output with those parameters.

How does a language model work?

A token is a small piece of text. The model turns tokens into numbers, relates them to the surrounding context using attention, and predicts what to generate next.

04 / Know the limitation

Confident ≠ correct.

✦

AI is excellent at producing language.

It uses learned parameters and context to generate likely sequences of tokens.

Fluent is not the same as factual.

◇

AI can still make things up.

It may give wrong facts, false quotations or invented references — sometimes with great confidence.

Checking is your responsibility.

⚡Use AI as a starting point for thinking, not the final authority.

Confident does not mean correct

AI can give invented references, mistaken calculations or claims that sound more certain than the evidence. Even linked sources may not fully support an answer.

Try this: Check the original source, not just the AI summary. For A-level work, compare with your textbook, lesson material and reliable subject sources.

06 / Two sides of the argument

Does Copilot make you a better learner?

Yes — when it supports your thinking

“It can explain difficult ideas, quiz me and give feedback. I still have to practise and decide what I believe.”

No — when it replaces your thinking

“If it produces all my answers, I may finish quicker but never learn how to do the work myself.”

The difference: Learning develops a skill you can use without AI; copying does not.

Are you actually learning?

AI is most useful when it leaves you more capable than before. If you always need Copilot to produce the answer, that is a sign to change how you are using it.

Try this: Try a five-minute, no-AI explanation of a topic. If you get stuck, ask Copilot to explain only the bit you missed.

Three believable mistakes

The Mathematics, Geography and Psychology responses on this slide are made-up examples of the sorts of errors AI might make. They are not measured results from Copilot.

Try this: Before trusting an answer, ask: Is the calculation correct? Is the factual claim sourced? Does the conclusion follow from the evidence?

08 / Check the answer

Three confident answers. Three mistakes.

%Mathematics
Wrong AI answer

“£100. The two changes cancel out.”

£96, not £100.

£100 → £120 → £96. The 20% decrease is taken from £120, not £100.

≈Geography
Wrong AI answer

“Twice as much energy.”

About 32 times the energy, not twice.

Earthquake magnitude is logarithmic: one whole-number step represents about 31.6× the energy.

↗Psychology
Wrong AI answer

“Social media causes anxiety.”

Correlation does not prove causation.

Anxiety might increase social-media use, or another factor could affect both.

Context is key. Check the working. Check the facts. Check the evidence.

What makes the three answers wrong?

Maths: 20% down from £120 gives £96. Geography: one full magnitude step is about 32 times the earthquake energy. Psychology: an association alone does not show what caused what.

Try this: Always show your working, confirm specialist facts, and avoid claiming a study 'proves' more than it actually does.

09 / Where is the line?

Use it wisely. Own your work.

Green / Helpful

Learn

  • Ask for explanations
  • Make practice questions
  • Compare different ideas

Amber / Check first

Improve

  • Feedback on your draft
  • Editing and rewriting
  • Using AI in assessed tasks

Red / Not OK

Misrepresent

  • Submit AI writing as your own
  • Invent sources or evidence
  • Share private or sensitive data

For assessment, follow your teacher's instructions and your school's AI and academic-integrity rules.

Green, amber or red?

Green means general learning support. Amber means ask your teacher before using AI for editing, feedback or assessed work. Red means passing off AI output as yours, making up evidence or exposing personal data.

Try this: When unsure, ask your teacher before using Copilot on the task. An approved tool is not automatically approved for every assessment.

10 / A fair question

Is using AI for homework cheating?

Not necessarily — when permitted

“If my teacher allows it, using Copilot to explain a method or test my recall can help me learn.”

Yes — if you misrepresent the work

“If I hand in generated writing or reasoning as my own, I've hidden what I can actually do.”

The deciding questions: Was it allowed? Was I honest? Can I explain it?

AI and homework

Using Copilot to help you understand a method may be fine if your teacher allows it. Handing in AI-written work as your own hides what you can really do.

Try this: Keep a record of any AI use when required, follow your teacher's instructions, and make sure you can explain every submitted claim.

Useful links: JCQ: what counts as AI misuse ↗

11 / The question to take away

If Copilot disappeared tomorrow, could you still explain your work?

Yes — I have learnt it

“I can explain my choices, defend my evidence and work through the problem without the tool.”

Not yet — I relied on it

“It helped me finish, but I can't explain why the answer works. I need to try again unaided.”

AI should make you a better thinker — not do your thinking for you.

The no-Copilot test

The best test of learning is whether you can explain your decisions without an AI tool next to you. You do not have to know everything, but you should know what is yours.

Try this: Close Copilot, pick one answer you wrote today, and explain why it works to someone else.

13 / AI-generated images

A fake image can cause real harm.

AI can make images look real. Creating or sharing a fake image of someone can have serious consequences.

Impersonation

Making it look as though someone did or said something they didn't.

Bullying

Making or sharing images to embarrass, threaten or humiliate someone.

Sexualised fakes

Never create, request or share intimate or sexualised images of a real person.

If an image targets someone: don't forward it. Tell a trusted member of school staff.

AI images can hurt real people

A fake or edited image of someone can be used to bully, impersonate or sexually exploit them. Do not create or share images that target another person.

Try this: If you encounter harmful content, do not forward, save or screenshot it. Tell the school safeguarding team or another trusted adult. If you are under 18, Childline's Report Remove can help with sexual images, including AI fakes.

14 / Your assessed work

Your NEA. Your work.

OCR  ·  Pearson Edexcel  ·  JCQ guidance

Revision and understanding

AI can help you learn.

“Explain this theory.”

“Quiz me on this topic.”

NEA / coursework submission

AI cannot be your author.

“Write my analysis.”

“Generate my conclusions.”

Before using AI in assessed work: ask your teacher, follow subject rules and acknowledge any permitted use.

NEA and coursework: be careful

For OCR and Pearson Edexcel assessments, your submission must demonstrate your own independent work. Even acknowledged AI assistance may not earn credit, and the rules depend on the subject and task.

Try this: Before using Copilot for any assessed work, ask your teacher what is permitted. Where it is allowed, keep the prompts and responses and acknowledge your use as required. Do not submit AI-written analysis or conclusions as your own.
