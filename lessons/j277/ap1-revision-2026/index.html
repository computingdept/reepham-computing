QUESTION 1 OF 4

CPU recall

Name the two main CPU components responsible for calculations and control, and describe what the fetch–decode–execute cycle does.

ANSWER

ALU performs arithmetic and logic; CU coordinates CPU operations. The fetch–decode–execute cycle retrieves an instruction, interprets it and carries it out.

QUESTION 2 OF 4

Number conversion

Convert denary 93 into 8-bit binary and two-digit hexadecimal.

ANSWER

01011101 and 5D. Split binary as 0101 1101 → 5 D.

QUESTION 3 OF 4

Memory and storage

Explain why solid-state storage is suitable for a tablet that is often moved.

ANSWER

No moving parts makes it more durable; fast access and small/light construction suit portability.

QUESTION 4 OF 4

Algorithm recognition

Which search checks items in order, and when does it stop?

ANSWER

Linear search: when the target is found OR when all items have been checked.

STEP 1 OF 16 · PAPER 1 · CPU TRINITY

The CPU trinity

ALU performs calculations and logic. CU coordinates and controls the fetch–decode–execute cycle. Cache stores frequently used data and instructions for rapid access.

Exam recall: clock speed, cache size and number of cores affect performance.

STEP 2 OF 16 · FIRST TEACHING · REGISTERS

CPU registers — four tiny stores

Registers are very small, very fast storage locations inside the CPU. They hold what the CPU needs immediately.

MARAddress of a memory location being accessed
MDRData or instruction being transferred
PCAddress of the next instruction
ACC · AccumulatorResults of calculations
2 of 16
STEP 3 OF 16 · REGISTER STORY

Follow one instruction

1. PC identifies the address of the next instruction. 2. MAR holds the address to access. 3. MDR holds the instruction/data transferred. 4. ACC can hold a result after processing.

Exam distinction: MAR and PC hold addresses; MDR and ACC hold data/results.

STEP 5 OF 16 · PAPER 1 · QUICK RECAP

Binary, hexadecimal, image and sound

Binary ↔ hex: split 8 bits into two groups of four. Bitmap size (bits): width × height × colour depth. Convert bits → bytes ÷ 8; bytes → KB ÷ 1000.

Sound: analogue wave → samples. Sample rate = samples per second (Hz); bit depth = bits per sample.

STEP 6 OF 16 · PAPER 2 · PRIOR KNOWLEDGE

Searching, sorting and abstraction

Linear: check each item until found or list ends. Bubble: compare neighbouring values and swap if in wrong order. Insertion: build a sorted section.

Abstraction: ignore irrelevant detail; keep information required to solve the problem.

STEP 13 OF 16 · PAPER 2 · DESIGN

A flowchart is an algorithm design

Oval: Start/End. Parallelogram: Input/Output. Rectangle: Process. Diamond: Decision with labelled Yes/No paths.

When asked to design an algorithm, pseudocode or a correct flowchart may be appropriate unless the question specifies a representation.

13 of 16
STEP 14 OF 16 · PAPER 2 · CODING

Validation supports robust design

Sequence: read the input, then process it, then output. Selection: use if … then … else … endif.

Validation: check sensible data against rules, e.g. age >= 8 AND age <= 75. A robust program handles valid and invalid inputs, can show an error message and ask again when required.

14 of 16
STEP 15 OF 16 · PAPER 2 · DEFENSIVE DESIGN

Robust and defensive design

Authentication verifies who the user is (such as checking credentials). This prevents unauthorised access to sensitive records.

Validation checks whether entered data is sensible; it does not establish identity.

15 of 16
STEP 16 OF 16 · PAPER 2 · TESTING

Test data and when to test

Normal: typical valid input. Erroneous: invalid input. Boundary: at or just outside a limit.

Iterative testing: test during development, then fix and retest.

Terminal testing: test the finished program at the end of development, before release.

Design a joining algorithm

On paper: pseudocode OR a flowchart. Match every decision branch to an output.
Teacher model
STEP 1 OF 4

Identify inputs and output

A swimming club accepts a member only if age is at least 12 AND the parent has agreed. Inputs: age, parentAgrees. Output: Accepted or Not accepted.

STEP 2 OF 4

Read two inputs

age = input("Age")
parentAgrees = input("Parent agreed? true/false")

STEP 3 OF 4

Use selection

if age >= 12 AND parentAgrees == true then
    print("Accepted")
else
    print("Not accepted")
endif

STEP 4 OF 4

Check the design

At 12 with permission → Accepted. At 11 → Not accepted. With no permission → Not accepted. You could draw the same logic as a decision diamond with Yes and No branches.

QUESTION 3 · ORIGINAL PRACTICE

Test data [3]

A counter accepts values from 100 to 500 inclusive. Supply one normal, one erroneous and one boundary test value. State expected acceptance/rejection.

WORKED ANSWER 3 OF 7

Normal: 250 → accepted. Erroneous: -10 → rejected. Boundary: 100 → accepted (or 500); 99 → rejected is an additional useful just-outside check.

QUESTION 4 · ORIGINAL PRACTICE

Iterative testing [2]

A developer changes the validation range after finding a defect. State when iterative testing is done and why.

WORKED ANSWER 4 OF 7

Testing takes place repeatedly during development, especially after code changes; it checks whether the fix works and helps find further errors.

QUESTION 5 · ORIGINAL PRACTICE

Authentication [2]

A community health club stores members' addresses. Explain how authentication is a defensive design measure.

WORKED ANSWER 5 OF 7

Requiring a username/password verifies the user's identity (1), so only authorised staff can access stored member addresses (1).

QUESTION 6 · ORIGINAL PRACTICE

Algorithms [3]

WORKED ANSWER 6 OF 7

After pass: [2, 7, 4, 9]. Linear search works on unsorted data.

QUESTION 7 · ORIGINAL PRACTICE

Abstraction and arrays [2]

A bus timetable app shows route numbers and arrival times but not the seat upholstery colour. Identify the computational-thinking principle. State what an array stores.

WORKED ANSWER 7 OF 7

Abstraction ignores irrelevant seat colour and retains useful journey details. An array stores multiple values under one name, accessed by index.
