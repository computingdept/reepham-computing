QUESTION 1 OF 4

Base case

What is a base case?

ANSWER

The condition that stops further recursive calls.

QUESTION 2 OF 4

Singleton

Why is a one-item list already sorted?

ANSWER

There is no second item that could be out of order.

QUESTION 3 OF 4

Split

What does it mean to split an array into two halves?

ANSWER

Create two smaller contiguous parts from the original data.

QUESTION 4 OF 4

Merge

What must be true before two lists can be merged in order efficiently?

ANSWER

Each input sublist should already be sorted.

Merge Sort

By the end, you can…
  • explain merge sort as a recursive divide-and-conquer algorithm;
  • demonstrate the split phase and the merge phase accurately;
  • trace how sorted sublists combine into the final sorted list.

TERM 1 OF 8

merge sort

DEFINITION

A divide-and-conquer sorting algorithm that recursively splits data and merges sorted sublists.

TERM 2 OF 8

divide and conquer

DEFINITION

Solve a problem by dividing it into smaller versions, solving them and combining the results.

TERM 3 OF 8

split

DEFINITION

Divide a list into smaller sublists.

TERM 4 OF 8

base case

DEFINITION

The condition that stops further recursive splitting.

TERM 5 OF 8

singleton

DEFINITION

A list containing exactly one item.

TERM 6 OF 8

merge

DEFINITION

Combine sorted sublists into one larger sorted list.

TERM 7 OF 8

sorted sublist

DEFINITION

A smaller list whose items are already in order.

TERM 8 OF 8

recursion

DEFINITION

A function solving a problem by calling itself on smaller versions of the problem.

STANDARD ALGORITHM · SORTING

Split down → merge up

7  4  2  6
↓ split
[7 4]   [2 6]
↓ split
[7] [4] [2] [6]
↓ merge
[4 7]   [2 6]
↓ merge
[2 4 6 7]
Key idea: The exact implementation of the merge routine is not required by the uploaded notes, but students must understand how the merge works.
STEP 1 OF 4

Split the input

7 4 2 6
→ [7 4] [2 6]

Divide the problem into smaller lists.

1 of 4
STEP 2 OF 4

Reach the base case

[7] [4] [2] [6]

Single-item lists are already sorted.

2 of 4
STEP 3 OF 4

Merge sorted pairs

[7] + [4] → [4 7]
[2] + [6] → [2 6]

Compare the front items and take the smaller one.

3 of 4
STEP 4 OF 4

Merge again

[4 7] + [2 6]
→ [2 4 6 7]

The final merge produces the sorted list.

SOURCE WORKED EXAMPLE

Trace 7, 4, 2, 6

7  4  2  6
Model the method clearly
STEP 1

Split into halves

[7 4]   [2 6]

Each half is still too large, so split again.

STEP 2

Reach singletons

[7] [4]   [2] [6]

The recursive base case is reached.

STEP 3

Merge pairs

[4 7]   [2 6]

Each pair is now sorted.

STEP 4

Final merge

compare 4 vs 2 → 2
compare 4 vs 6 → 4
compare 7 vs 6 → 6
append 7
→ [2 4 6 7]

The full list is sorted.
