NEW LEARNING · ONE SHARED SCENARIO

Should the school introduce the proposed AI monitoring?

A fictional school proposes AI monitoring of activity on school-managed devices. It would flag potentially concerning activity for trained safeguarding staff. Staff would check context before acting; the proposal does not include automatic sanctions.

Reasoning prompts

  1. Point: who could benefit or be harmed?
  2. Explain: why might this happen?
  3. Consequence: what could follow?
  4. Challenge: what weakens the argument?
  5. Judgement: which option is justified, and when?
STEP 1 OF 5

Knowledge before judgement

An alert is an estimate, not proof. False positives flag harmless activity; false negatives miss genuine concerns. Overall accuracy can hide different error rates for different groups.

STEP 2 OF 5

Privacy and fairness

Monitoring can reveal sensitive activity. Limit collection and access to what the purpose requires. Biased training data or poor context can make particular groups more likely to be flagged unfairly.

STEP 3 OF 5

People and work

Automating the first scan could save staff time. Checking alerts, training and appeals also require time and skills. Work may shift towards review rather than simply disappear.

STEP 4 OF 5

Develop the reasoning

Name a stakeholder, explain the mechanism, then show a consequence. “It saves time” is a point; “staff can review a concern sooner, so a pupil may receive earlier support” develops it.

STEP 5 OF 5

Weigh and decide

Ask what weakens the benefit: false alerts, staff capacity or intrusive collection. Judge which matters most here and give conditions. Legal compliance alone does not settle the ethical question.

I DO · BUILD ONE ARGUMENT

Should the school introduce the proposed AI monitoring?

A fictional school proposes AI monitoring of activity on school-managed devices. It would flag potentially concerning activity for trained safeguarding staff. Staff would check context before acting; the proposal does not include automatic sanctions.

Follow how each sentence adds something new. A different conclusion can be strong if its reasoning fits the evidence.

STEP 1 OF 6

Point

Pupils could benefit from earlier safeguarding support.

Why this develops the argument

Name who benefits and the specific benefit; “AI is useful” would be too vague.

STEP 2 OF 6

Explain

The system could flag concerning activity sooner than staff manually reviewing the same activity.

Why this develops the argument

Give a plausible mechanism. “Could” recognises that this benefit needs evidence rather than being guaranteed.

STEP 3 OF 6

Consequence

If trained staff check those alerts promptly, they could offer help before a concern becomes more serious.

Why this develops the argument

Develop the impact on pupils and state the condition on which the benefit depends.

STEP 4 OF 6

Challenge

However, legitimate research could trigger false alerts, distressing pupils and diverting staff from genuine concerns.

Why this develops the argument

Challenge the benefit with a specific competing consequence, rather than adding an unrelated disadvantage.

STEP 5 OF 6

Judgement

On balance, I would support a limited, reviewed trial only if staff can check alerts promptly, collection is restricted and pupils can challenge mistakes; otherwise, I would delay introduction.

Why this develops the argument

Weigh benefit against risk and make the decision conditional. Human review helps but does not guarantee fairness.

STEP 6 OF 6

The complete model

Pupils could benefit from earlier safeguarding support. The system could flag concerning activity sooner than staff manually reviewing the same activity. If trained staff check those alerts promptly, they could offer help before a concern becomes more serious. However, legitimate research could trigger false alerts, distressing pupils and diverting staff from genuine concerns. On balance, I would support a limited, reviewed trial only if staff can check alerts promptly, collection is restricted and pupils can challenge mistakes; otherwise, I would delay introduction.

Point → explanation → consequence → counterargument → justified judgement. Use these as prompts, not a compulsory paragraph formula.

WE DO · CHANGE ONE CONDITION

Should the school keep every browsing history?

The school now wants to keep a full browsing history for every pupil for two years. Safeguarding staff could search it later. The school has not shown that keeping everything is necessary. Staff would still review alerts before acting.

Choose A or B on mini-whiteboards. Explain why the other sentence is weaker before revealing the response.

DECISION 1 OF 5 · CHOOSE

Choose a relevant point

A. Keeping every history could intrude on pupils’ privacy.

B. Keeping every history must make pupils feel safer.

DECISION 1 OF 5 · REVIEW

Stronger choice: A

Keeping every browsing history could intrude on pupils’ privacy.

Why?

A identifies a plausible risk. B assumes that all pupils will feel safer without evidence.

DECISION 2 OF 5 · CHOOSE

Choose an explanation

A. Privacy matters because privacy is important to pupils.

B. A full history could reveal sensitive interests beyond a concern.

DECISION 2 OF 5 · REVIEW

Stronger choice: B

Full histories could reveal sensitive interests beyond a particular safeguarding concern.

Why?

B explains what creates the risk. A repeats the point without explaining it.

DECISION 3 OF 5 · CHOOSE

Choose a consequence

A. Pupils may avoid legitimate research if they fear misinterpretation.

B. Pupils will certainly stop using every school device.

DECISION 3 OF 5 · REVIEW

Stronger choice: A

Pupils might avoid legitimate research if they fear that staff will misinterpret it.

Why?

A gives a plausible behavioural consequence. B makes an unsupported prediction about every pupil.

DECISION 4 OF 5 · CHOOSE

Choose a fair counterargument

A. Long retention is acceptable because more data is always better.

B. A retained history could help staff investigate a later concern.

DECISION 4 OF 5 · REVIEW

Stronger choice: B

However, a retained history could help staff understand a concern reported later.

Why?

B recognises a relevant benefit without claiming it justifies unlimited collection. A assumes more data must be better.

DECISION 5 OF 5 · CHOOSE

Choose a justified judgement

A. Reject the two-year full-history plan unless its need is shown; assess a narrower option.

B. Reject every monitoring tool because privacy always outweighs safeguarding.

DECISION 5 OF 5 · REVIEW

Stronger choice: A

I would reject the full-history plan as proposed: the possible investigative benefit does not yet justify keeping everything for two years. Assess a narrower, justified collection and retention approach instead.

Why?

A weighs the proposal’s benefit against its specific privacy cost. B makes an absolute claim without weighing this scenario.

BRING THE REASONING TOGETHER

Our model argument

Keeping a full browsing history could intrude on pupils’ privacy because it may reveal sensitive interests beyond a particular concern. Pupils might avoid legitimate research if they fear misinterpretation. However, retained histories could help staff investigate concerns reported later. I would reject this plan as proposed: that possible benefit does not yet justify keeping everything for two years. The school should assess a narrower approach and justify its collection, access and retention.

A different judgement needs to explain why the benefit outweighs the privacy cost under stated conditions.

FOUNDATION · QUESTION 1 OF 3

Return to the original proposal: trained staff check alerts and there are no automatic sanctions. Write three sentences developing one benefit for pupils. Use: point → why → consequence.

ONE POSSIBLE DEVELOPED ANSWER

Pupils could receive earlier safeguarding support. The system could identify concerning activity sooner than a manual review of the same activity. If trained staff check promptly and accurately, pupils could receive help before a concern escalates.

Check: name a stakeholder; explain a mechanism; add a consequence. Do not repeat “it helps pupils” three ways.

CORE · QUESTION 2 OF 3

Changed proposal: an alert now triggers an automatic detention; a pupil can appeal afterwards. Evaluate this change in a short paragraph. Develop a benefit, challenge it and reach a justified judgement.

ONE POSSIBLE DEVELOPED ANSWER

Automatic detention could reduce the time staff spend deciding initial sanctions. However, legitimate research could trigger a false alert, so a pupil may be punished unfairly and lose trust before an appeal is heard. I would reject automatic detention on an alert alone: review before action matters more than this time saving. A later appeal does not undo all of the initial harm.

Check: explain both effects, weigh their importance and judge this change. Other supported conclusions are possible; an alert alone is not proof.

CHALLENGE · TRANSFER · QUESTION 3 OF 3

An employer proposes AI shortlisting trained on past hiring decisions. It would reject applicants with career breaks without review. Evaluate the proposal in a short paragraph, considering applicants and recruitment staff. Use your own structure.

ONE POSSIBLE DEVELOPED ANSWER

Automated shortlisting could help recruiters process applications faster, leaving time for interviews. However, past hiring decisions may contain bias, and career breaks do not establish a lack of skill. Rejecting these applicants without review could exclude capable people unfairly. I would not approve this design: the speed benefit does not justify an unexplained exclusion. Assess job-relevant evidence, audit outcomes and offer meaningful human review before relying on it.

Check: transfer the reasoning to recruitment; develop effects on both groups; justify a decision. “AI is biased” alone does not explain the mechanism or consequence.

TERM 1 OF 5

stakeholder

A person or group affected by a decision.

TERM 2 OF 5

false positive

An alert that incorrectly treats harmless activity as concerning.

TERM 3 OF 5

bias

Systematic distortion that can produce unfair outcomes.

TERM 4 OF 5

counterargument

A reason that challenges or limits an argument.

TERM 5 OF 5

justified judgement

A decision supported by reasons that weigh the alternatives.

True or false?

“The system is fast. However, it could invade privacy. Therefore, use it.” is already a well-justified argument.

Model answer

False. It names competing points but does not explain their consequences or why one should carry more weight. A judgement needs reasons tied to this proposal.
