NEW LEARNING · ALGORITHM FIRST

Compare two heuristic choices

// A*: f(n) = g(n) + h(n)
// An admissible h(n) NEVER exceeds
// the actual least cost to the goal.
// With a bad overestimate, early-goal
// termination may produce a nonoptimal path.
STEP 1 OF 5

Find the real remaining cost

From A, the cheapest route A→B→G costs 2+2=4. Therefore h(A)=3 is admissible, but h(A)=9 is not.

STEP 2 OF 5

Safe estimates support optimal search

With h(A)=3, A is expanded before B, improving the route to B and finally G at cost 6.

STEP 3 OF 5

Watch the misleading estimate

With h(A)=9, A has f=11; B has f=7 and leads to goal at cost 8.

STEP 4 OF 5

Why the error matters

A* that stops on the first goal removed from the open set can return this 8-cost path before exploring A, even though a 6-cost route exists.

STEP 5 OF 5

Evaluate, do not just calculate

State both the numeric result and whether the supplied heuristic guarantees optimality under the algorithm's assumptions.

I DO · SAFE HEURISTIC

Check admissibility and trace U→G with safe estimates.

Edges U–V=1, U–W=5, V–W=2, V–G=7, W–G=2.
h(U)=4, h(V)=3, h(W)=1, h(G)=0.
STEP 1 OF 4

Predict the choice

V has g1,h3,f4; W has g5,h1,f6. Expand V.

STEP 2 OF 4

Review the choice

V improves W to g3 and f4; expand W.

STEP 3 OF 4

Review the choice

W improves the goal G to g5,h0,f5.

STEP 4 OF 4

Review the choice

The path is U→V→W→G with total cost 5; the heuristics do not exceed the actual remaining costs.

WE DO · MISLEADING HEURISTIC

Can a bad estimate make A* return a longer route?

Edges M–N=2, M–O=5, N–O=2, N–G=7, O–G=2.
h(M)=4,h(N)=7 (unsafe),h(O)=1,h(G)=0.
STEP 1 OF 4

Predict the choice

After M, O has f5+1=6; N has f2+7=9, so O is selected.

STEP 2 OF 4

Review the choice

O reaches G at g7,f7. G is selected before N, so the early-goal algorithm returns cost 7.

STEP 3 OF 4

Review the choice

But M→N→O→G costs 2+2+2=6; the bad h(N)=7 overestimates N's true remaining cost 4.

STEP 4 OF 4

Review the choice

Using an admissible estimate such as h(N)=3 allows the cheaper path to be found.

Foundation · 4 marks · Q1/3

The true remaining distance from node A to goal G is 6. Decide whether h(A)=4 and h(A)=8 are admissible, with reasons.

WORKED ANSWER

[4] h(A)=4 is admissible [1] because it does not exceed 6 [1]; h(A)=8 is inadmissible [1] because it overestimates 6 [1].

Core · 6 marks · Q2/3

Edges S–A=2, S–B=6, A–B=2, B–G=2, A–G=8. Calculate the best S→G route and explain why h(A)=9 can mislead A*.

WORKED ANSWER

[6] Route S→A→B→G [2], cost 6 [1]; from A the true cheapest remaining cost is 4 [1], so h(A)=9 overestimates [1] and can delay expanding A while a more expensive goal route appears [1].

Stretch · 6 marks · Q3/3

Compare Dijkstra's algorithm and A* in their selection rule, heuristic use and guarantee of shortest paths.

WORKED ANSWER

[6] Dijkstra selects lowest tentative g [1] and does not need h [1]; A* selects lowest f=g+h [2]; Dijkstra needs non-negative edges [1]; A* requires suitable admissible heuristic (and appropriate closed-set handling, usually consistency) to guarantee optimality [1].

TERM 1 OF 5

admissible

Heuristic never overestimates the true cheapest remaining cost.

TERM 2 OF 5

consistent

Heuristic obeys h(u) ≤ edge(u,v)+h(v) for every edge.

TERM 3 OF 5

suboptimal

A valid route whose cost is greater than the cheapest possible.

TERM 4 OF 5

frontier

Discovered vertices awaiting expansion.

TERM 5 OF 5

heuristic

An estimated remaining cost used to guide search.

True or false?

If a heuristic overestimates the true remaining cost, A* may lose its shortest-route guarantee.

Model answer

True. An inadmissible estimate can make a less costly path appear too unattractive to explore before stopping.

Confidence check

Can you distinguish an admissible estimate from an overestimate?
